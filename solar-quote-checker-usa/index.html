Questions to ask your installer

    How the check works

    1. Price per watt: your total price divided by your total panel watts. In November 2026 the US average was $2.60/W (EnergySage marketplace quotes, before incentives) — from $2.12/W in Florida to $3.71/W in Alabama. A competitive cash price is about $2.50–$3.50/W in most of the country. The tool compares your quote with your state's average, allowing about 15% below and 20% above it.
    2. Loans cost more: a solar loan usually adds about $0.60/W to the price — the lender's dealer fee is rolled into the loan balance. So a loan quote is judged against a range $0.60/W higher than a cash quote. Always ask for the cash price too, and the loan APR.
    3. The 30% tax credit is DEAD for homeowners. The federal Residential Clean Energy Credit (Section 25D) ended for systems installed after December 31, 2025 (One Big Beautiful Bill Act, Public Law 119-21). If a 2026 quote subtracts a 30% credit from your price, the real cost is thousands more than shown — the tool adds it back and judges the real price. Leases and PPAs are different: the company that owns the system can still claim a credit through December 31, 2027.
    4. Battery adder: a home battery installed with solar costs about $1,000–$1,300 per kWh in 2026 — a Tesla Powerwall 3 (13.5 kWh) typically runs $13,500–$16,500 installed. That is added to the fair range above.
    5. Production check: a US home system makes about 1,100–1,600 kWh per kW per year (more in the sunny Southwest, less in the cloudy North). If your quote promises far more than that, the production number is inflated — the #1 trick in solar proposals.
    6. Panel-to-inverter ratio: total panel kW divided by inverter kW. 1.0–1.3 is normal — a little more panel than inverter is good. Above 1.4 the inverter clips power on sunny days; below 0.9 you paid for inverter capacity you will not use.

    Questions

    My quote is much cheaper than the fair range. Is that good?

    Not always. A very cheap quote often means lower-grade panels, a no-name inverter, a thin roof mount, or items left out (permits, interconnection, wiring). Ask the installer to put the panel brand, model and warranty in writing, and compare two more quotes before you decide.

    Cash or loan — which is better?

    Cash is almost always cheaper: loans add about $0.60/W in dealer fees, and the total you repay over 20–25 years can be far more than the system price. If you must finance, ask for the cash price and the loan price side by side, the APR, and the dealer fee as a dollar amount — not just the monthly payment.

    What about the 30% federal tax credit?

    For homeowners who buy (cash or loan), it ended December 31, 2025. A 2026 quote that still shows it is either out of date or misleading you — the real price is about 43% higher than the "after credit" number shown. Only leases and PPAs still get a federal credit, because the company owns the system and claims it through 2027.

    Lease or PPA — should I take one?

    You pay nothing up front, but you do not own the panels, you do not get the tax credit, and the monthly rate usually rises every year (an escalator, often 2–3%). Compare the lease rate with your current utility rate, ask what happens if you sell the house, and get a cash purchase quote for the same system so you can compare honestly.

    What should be in writing before I sign anything?

    Panel brand, model, wattage and warranty; inverter brand, model and warranty; battery brand, capacity and warranty; roof condition; what installation includes (permits, interconnection, wiring); the yearly production estimate; and, if financing, the APR and dealer fee. If it is not written down, it is not promised.
